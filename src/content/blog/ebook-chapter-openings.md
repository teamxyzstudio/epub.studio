You can usually tell an auto-converted ebook from a typeset one within a paragraph, and it is rarely the body text that gives it away. It is the chapter opening.

## Do not indent the first line

A first-line indent exists to signal "this is a new paragraph, continuing from the last." The first paragraph after a heading has nothing to continue from, so the indent is meaningless — and a well-set book never has one there.

This is the most common tell in converted files, because a naive converter applies the indent uniformly.

## Pick indents *or* spacing

An indent and a blank line between paragraphs solve the same problem twice. Fiction conventionally uses indents; non-fiction and technical writing often use spacing. Using both reads as a document rather than a book.

## Drop caps, carefully

A drop cap is a lovely signal that a chapter has begun. It is also the single easiest thing to get wrong in a reflowable format, because the letter has to align to a text block whose size the reader controls.

Two rules: three lines deep is plenty, and the rest of the first line should not be indented behind it. If you cannot preview it at several font sizes, skip it — a bad drop cap is far worse than none.

## Let headings breathe

Space above a heading should exceed space below it. The gap belongs to the section starting, not the one ending — when they are equal, headings float ambiguously between the blocks they separate.

## Why the first line rule exists

The indent is a signal, and like any signal it means nothing if it fires constantly. Its job is to say "a new paragraph starts here, continuing the passage above". After a heading there is no passage above — the heading already did that work — so the indent is redundant and reads as an error to anyone who has looked at a lot of books.

This is one of those conventions that seems fussy until you see it broken, at which point the page looks subtly amateur without you being able to say why.

The same logic applies after a scene break, after a block quote, and after an image. In every case something has already announced the break.

## Indents or spacing, never both

Both solve the same problem: showing where one paragraph ends and the next begins. Using both is redundancy that reads as uncertainty.

Convention, loosely:

- **Indents** for fiction and narrative non-fiction. Compact, traditional, keeps the text block dense and even.
- **Spacing** for technical writing, documentation, anything read in fragments rather than continuously. Easier to scan, easier to re-enter after looking away.

Indent depth wants to be about one em — roughly the width of the letter M at your body size. Much less and it does not register; much more and it punches a visible hole in the left margin.

## Drop caps, honestly

A drop cap is the hardest thing on this list to get right in a reflowable book, because it has to align to a text block whose size the reader controls.

If you use one:

- **Three lines deep.** Two looks tentative, four starts colliding with everything.
- **No indent on that paragraph.** The cap is already the signal.
- **Watch the letter.** A, W and V leave awkward gaps; quotation marks preceding the first letter are a known nightmare.
- **Test at both extremes** of text size before shipping.

If you cannot test it properly, skip it. A missing drop cap is invisible; a broken one is the first thing anyone sees.

## Heading space is asymmetric

Space above a heading should exceed space below it, usually by around two to one. The gap belongs to the section that is beginning, and grouping the heading with the text it introduces is what makes the structure legible at a glance.

When the space is equal, headings float between blocks and the reader has to actually read them to work out which side they belong to. It is a small thing that makes a document feel disorganised without being obviously wrong.

## Where this matters most

Chapter openings are read more attentively than anything else in a book. A reader arriving at a new chapter is orienting — they look at the heading, the first line, the shape of the page. Every flaw is maximally visible at exactly that moment, and it sets the expectation for everything that follows.

The rest of the book is read in flow, where small inconsistencies pass unnoticed. So if you only have the patience to fix one thing, fix the chapter openings.
